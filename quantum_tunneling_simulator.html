4. The approximation being used

T ≈ e−2κd   ·   κ = √[2m(V₀ − E)] / ℏ

When E < V₀, the amplitude envelope |ψ| falls as e−κx within the barrier; its relative probability density |ψ|² falls as e−2κx. The single-energy density profile does not move with time.

At a distance 1/κ inside the barrier, amplitude is 1/e ≈ 36.8% of its value at the start, while relative density is 1/e² ≈ 13.5%. The density's own 1/e distance is 1/(2κ).

Deliberately simplified: neglects matching factors and interference at the two boundaries, calculates no reflected wave, and treats the transmitted amplitude as √T times the incident amplitude. It is especially unreliable near the barrier top.

Why logarithms?

Following the calculation

1. Calculate kinetic energy

E = ½mv² = 187.5 J

2. Determine the decay constant

κ = √[2m(V₀ − E)] / ℏ

κ = 1.64 × 10³⁷ m⁻¹

3. Exponentiate without underflow

T ≈ e−2κd and ln(T) = −2κd

ln(T) = −9.82 × 10³⁵

4. Convert to base 10

log₁₀(T) = ln(T) / ln(10)

log₁₀(T) = −4.26 × 10³⁵

This extends the same toy single-particle formula to a whole person's center of mass. A real person is not a coherent, isolated single particle; internal structure and environmental decoherence are omitted, along with boundary matching and reflected waves. The result is a mathematical illustration, not a realistic probability of walking through a door.
This is a conceptual teaching simulator, not an exact Schrödinger-equation solution. The plots depict idealized envelopes, not a spatially normalized probability distribution. The door example is not a physically realistic model of a person. The animated real-part trace, when enabled, slows phase evolution for visibility; it is not a moving wave packet. No internet connection, external libraries, or installation is needed.
